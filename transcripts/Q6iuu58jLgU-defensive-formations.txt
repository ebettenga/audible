## 6-2

6-2. This formation involves six defensive linemen and two linebackers, hence the name 6-2. There are also three defensive backs, either safeties or cornerbacks, for a total of 11 defensive players. The 6'2 is run defense at its most extreme. It works because these six linemen create gap control. They plug every hole between offensive linemen. These are called A gaps between center and guards, B gaps between guards and tackles, and C gaps outside the tackles. It's mathematically impossible for a running back to find space if the defensive linemen win their battles against the offensive line. But with only three defensive backs covering potentially four or five receivers, the offense will almost certainly have an uncovered receiver if they choose to pass. The corners are isolated on islands with no help over the top. A quick slant pass or any route over 10 yards becomes a likely completion. The Rams under Shawn McVey use it in goal line situations, especially against runheavy opponents like the 49ers. The Chargers use it too when they need to stop short yardage runs. These teams in particular like to use it because they have personnel advantages at the lineman position. The Rams have dominant interior linemen like Aaron Donald before retirement and the Chargers have defensive line depth. You'll see it on third and one, fourth and one goal line stands or when the offense needs two yards or less and will almost certainly run.

## 5-3

5-3. This formation involves five linemen, three linebackers, and three defensive backs. There is one less lineman than the 6-2 formation, but one more linebacker. This extra linebacker provides slightly better pass coverage while still maintaining solid run defense. The middle linebacker can drop into coverage on playaction passes and make tackles on outside runs. Playaction passes are when the quarterback fakes a handoff to trick linebackers into coming forward, but then drops back into the pocket to make a pass instead. The three defensive backs in this formation are a poor match if the offense has four or five receivers because the fourth and fifth receivers will need to be covered by a linebacker. This is a disadvantage for the defense due to the elite speed and agility that most receivers have. Smart quarterbacks will recognize this mismatch pre- snap and exploit it by targeting these receivers. However, the extra linebacker gives the defense someone who can cover a tight end on short routes. Every NFL team has 53 in their goal line package. The Eagles and Steelers use it most frequently because they have elite middle linebackers. Nakobe Dean for the Eagles and Patrick Queen for the Steelers are excellent at reading plays quickly and providing that crucial pass coverage.

## 52 Eagle

## 34

34. This formation involves three linemen, four linebackers, and four defensive backs. This is where football becomes psychological warfare. These four linebackers can do anything before the snap. ## 46 Bear

46 Bear. This formation involves five defensive linemen, three linebackers, one safety, and two cornerbacks, and is named after the Bears safety Doug Plank's jersey number 46. Three linemen line up directly over the center and both guards. This is called the bare front. It creates immediate pressure up the middle while the other two linemen rush from the edges. The linebackers crowd the line of scrimmage making it look like eight guys are coming. This formation was highly effective in the 80s because quarterbacks of that era operated primarily from under center with slower developing plays. The 46 bear created instant pressure before those plays could develop. The single safety would often blitz, bringing six or seven rushers that offensive lines couldn't handle. Modern defenses don't use it much because a quick three-step passing game with a bubble screen would likely convert for huge yards. With only two cornerbacks, one safety, and linebackers on the line of scrimmage sending pressure, any offense with four receivers would have at least one player running free with other quick pass options available with big play potential. The 1985 Bears made it famous, but the Ravens under Rex Ryan occasionally brought it back for specific situations. The Jets also used it when Ryan was their coordinator.

## Nickel

Nickel. This formation involves four linemen, two linebackers, and five defensive backs. The fifth defensive back is called the nickel corner. Nickel as in 5 cents, as in five defensive backs. The nickel corner is a defensive back who specializes in covering the slot receiver. That's the receiver that lines up inside the outermost receiver when the offense uses three or more receivers. Modern NFL offenses use three or more receivers on 65% of their plays. With traditional 4,3 or 34 defenses, that inside receiver is covered by a linebacker. Like we learned before, this is a poor matchup for defenses because linebackers run 4.7 second 40s while slot receivers run 4.4. The math doesn't add up in the defense's favor. That nickel corner will sometimes jam the slot receiver at the line of scrimmage, disrupting timing or play robber coverage. This is where they sit in passing lanes waiting to intercept balls thrown to predictable routes. The Cowboys have one of the best nickel corners in Trevan Diggs, who you'll often see playing robber coverage that leads to game-breaking picks, and the Bills use it as their base defense because they have great depth at defensive back. For teams with less depth at defensive back, an injury might require them to adjust a game plan that involved a lot of nickel coverage. These teams have a nickel advantage because they've invested in five or more quality defensive backs, which most teams don't have. However, with only two linebackers, inside running plays can break big if the offensive linemen win their blocks and allow the running back to get into the secondary. Due to their smaller size, nickel corners usually aren't great tacklers against 220-lb running backs.

## Dime
